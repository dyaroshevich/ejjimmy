Every second enquiry we get in Krabi includes the same sentence: "I want to sell the excess back to PEA." It is a reasonable wish and, in Thailand, a misleading one. Here is the honest version.

## The short version

Thailand does not have a simple, nationwide net-metering scheme where every unit you export is credited at the retail tariff. Small export-credit arrangements exist in some PEA and MEA service areas, they are quota-limited, the credit is well below the retail rate, and the rules change. Design a system that pays for itself on what you consume, and treat any export credit as a bonus.

## Why self-consumption is the whole game

Your bill is avoided at roughly ฿4.2–4.5 per unit. A credit for exported units is typically a fraction of that, when it exists at all. So the economics are driven by one question: how much of your production lands on a load you would otherwise pay for?

That means:

- Air conditioning running in the afternoon is the best load in a Thai house.
- A pool pump moved to 10:00–15:00 is free savings.
- A household that is empty all day saves far less from the same array — and a battery only helps if the evening load is large enough to justify ฿12,000–฿20,000 per kWh.

A 5 kWp array in Krabi produces roughly 550–600 kWh a month. If 60% is self-consumed, you save about ฿1,500 a month; if 25% is, you save about ฿600. Same panels, same price, completely different project.

## Grid-tie, hybrid or off-grid

**Grid-tie** is the default for a normal house with a stable supply. Cheapest per kW, no battery, no maintenance beyond cleaning. It shuts down during an outage — that is a safety requirement, not a design flaw.

**Hybrid** adds a battery and a backup circuit. It makes sense in three situations: the supply in your area drops often enough to matter, you have equipment that must not stop (pumps, freezers, medical devices), or you want evening independence and accept a longer payback. It does not make sense as a pure financial play on today's tariffs.

**Off-grid** is for properties the grid never reached, or where a connection quote is absurd. It is 2–4× the cost per usable kWh and demands disciplined load design. On a Krabi hillside with a 300 m cable run, it can still be the right answer.

## The connection process, realistically

1. **Application** with your installer's single-line diagram and equipment datasheets.
2. **Technical review** by the local PEA office — this is where single-phase service limits, transformer capacity and export approval are decided.
3. **Meter work.** If export is approved you get a two-way meter; if not, the existing meter stays and the inverter is set to zero-export.
4. **Inspection and commissioning**, then the system is signed off.

Typical residential single-phase service caps export around 5 kW. Three-phase opens more headroom. If you are planning 10 kWp or more, mention it at the survey stage — retrofitting three-phase after installation is expensive.

## Three things people get wrong

1. **Sizing for export.** A 10 kWp array on a 4,000 baht bill does not "earn" money; it idles.
2. **Assuming the meter is free.** Meter replacement, service upgrades and inspection fees are real line items.
3. **Believing the inverter can be switched to export later.** Some can, some are locked at commissioning. Decide before you buy.

## What we ask before quoting

Your last three bills, whether the house is occupied during the day, and whether the supply has ever dropped for more than a few minutes. Those three answers decide the system size and whether a battery belongs in it — not the size of your roof.
