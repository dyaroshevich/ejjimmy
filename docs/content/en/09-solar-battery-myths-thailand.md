Battery prices have fallen far enough that storage is now a normal part of a quote rather than an exotic add-on. That is good. The sales arguments around it have not improved at the same rate.

Here are the five claims we are asked about most, and what they are worth.

## Myth 1 — "The battery pays for itself"

In Thailand, most households pay a flat residential tariff. There is no meaningful time-of-use spread to arbitrage, so a battery cannot buy cheap and sell dear. Every stored unit is a unit you already generated, and its value is what you avoided paying — roughly ฿4.2–4.5 — minus the round-trip losses of 8–12%.

Do the arithmetic on a 10 kWh battery at ฿150,000: even in ideal use, it moves a few hundred baht of value a month. It does not repay itself inside its warranty on tariff savings.

What it does buy is continuity. If that is what you want, buy it — but buy it as insurance, not as an investment.

## Myth 2 — "With a battery I'm independent from PEA"

A hybrid system stays connected to the grid. The battery carries selected circuits through an outage; it does not run the whole house indefinitely, and the inverter still needs the grid to synchronise when it returns.

Real independence means off-grid: oversized array, large battery bank, load discipline, and a generator for the wet season. It costs two to four times more for the same comfort, and almost nobody with a PEA connection actually wants it once they see the number.

## Myth 3 — "Lead-acid is safer and cheaper"

Cheaper per nameplate kWh, yes. Cheaper per usable kWh over ten years, no — lead-acid tolerates only 50% depth of discharge, needs ventilation, watering in flooded types, and replacement every three to five years. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) takes 80–90% depth of discharge, needs no maintenance, and is thermally far more stable than the lithium chemistries people worry about.

For a home in a hot climate, LFP is the right default. The old argument belongs to 2015.

## Myth 4 — "I'll add the battery later"

Sometimes true, often not. It depends on the inverter you buy now:

- A **grid-tie** inverter cannot take a battery at all, ever.
- A **hybrid** inverter can, but only with a battery of the right voltage class and, often, the same brand. Compatibility lists change; five years from now the model may be discontinued.
- A **battery-ready hybrid** with the correct DC voltage is the cheapest path — if you choose it at the start.

So "later" is a decision you make today: either pay the hybrid premium now, or accept that storage means replacing the inverter too.

## Myth 5 — "It's a 10 kWh battery, so I have 10 kWh"

Usable capacity is lower than nameplate: 80–90% depth of discharge, minus inverter conversion losses, minus the reserve the system keeps for backup. A "10 kWh" unit gives you roughly 8 kWh at the socket, and less in the last years of its life as capacity fades — typically 2–3% a year.

This matters most when someone sizes a battery to a specific outage scenario, like "run the bedroom AC all night". Do that sum in usable kWh, or you will discover the gap at 2 a.m. in the middle of a storm.

## The questions that actually decide it

1. How often does the supply drop, and for how long?
2. Which loads must keep running, and for how many hours?
3. What is the evening consumption, in kWh, from the bill?
4. Is there any load that cannot tolerate a 20 ms transfer?
5. What is the budget ceiling, and is the battery competing with more panels?

Answer those five and the battery size falls out. Answer none of them and you will be sold whatever is on the shelf — which is exactly how a ฿150,000 ornament gets installed in a house that loses power twice a year for ten minutes.
