A solar pump is the simplest photovoltaic system there is: panels, a controller, a motor. No inverter, no utility application, no meter, no battery in most designs. Water is the battery — you pump into a tank when the sun is up and use it later.

For a Krabi garden, a farm, or a pool that runs all day, it is often the best-value kilowatt on the property.

## What it costs

Rough installed ranges, including panels, controller, pump and basic plumbing:

| Duty | Typical cost |
|---|---|
| Garden irrigation, 1–2 m³/h, 10–20 m head | ฿25,000 – ฿60,000 |
| Borehole, 3–5 m³/h, 40–80 m head | ฿80,000 – ฿180,000 |
| Pool circulation, 8–12 m³/h, low head | ฿45,000 – ฿90,000 |

Compare with a grid pump: the pump itself is cheaper, but you pay for the electricity forever, and on a large garden that is a real monthly line. For a pool running six hours a day, a solar circulation pump typically pays back in four to six years — and it keeps working while the rest of the house is dark.

## The three mistakes we see

**1. Sizing on flow instead of head.** A pump rated 5 m³/h at 10 m of head may deliver almost nothing at 60 m. Borehole depth plus pipe friction plus the height of the tank is the number that matters. Get a static water level reading, not the depth the driller wrote on the invoice.

**2. No dry-run protection.** This is the number one killer. The well drops in April, the pump keeps spinning, the motor cooks. A controller with dry-run detection costs a fraction of a replacement pump.

**3. Ignoring the tank.** A solar pump without storage gives you water only at midday. With a tank, the same system serves the garden in the evening. The tank is usually the cheapest part of the project and the one that makes it useful.

## Garden and irrigation

For a typical Krabi garden of a few rai, a 1.5–3 kWp array driving a 1–2 m³/h pump into an elevated tank covers drip irrigation comfortably. Panels can go on the pump house roof, on a small ground frame, or on a shade structure over the beds — the last one is free real estate and keeps the panels cooler.

Run irrigation in the morning and evening from the tank. Do not try to match pump output to sprinkler demand directly; the pressure swings with cloud.

## Pool circulation

A pool pump is a low-head, high-flow duty — a different pump type from a borehole unit. Running circulation on solar makes sense because the load is predictable and coincident with generation: the pool needs filtering during the day anyway.

Keep the grid pump as a backup, or keep a small cartridge pump for the wet season. A week of monsoon cloud will not maintain water clarity on solar alone, and that is the week guests complain.

## Boreholes and farms

For agriculture the arithmetic changes completely: pumping diesel or paying a farm tariff for hours a day is expensive, and the solar pump runs whenever the sun is out without supervision. The design constraint is the well's yield, not the array — an oversized array on a low-yield well just cycles the pump.

For a borehole, insist on: static and dynamic water level, yield test, and a controller that logs run hours. Those three numbers decide the pump, and no catalogue can substitute for them.

## What a solar pump is not

- It is not a whole-house water supply in the evening without a tank.
- It is not immune to lightning. Inland Krabi storms find pump controllers; surge protection on the DC side is not optional.
- It is not maintenance-free in salt air if the frame is mild steel.

## Start with the well, not the panel

Tell us the duty (how many cubic metres a day), the head, and whether you have storage. If you do not know the head, that is the first measurement — everything else is arithmetic.
