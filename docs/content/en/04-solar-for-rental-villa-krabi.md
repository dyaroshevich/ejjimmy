Rooftop solar on a rental property has one structural problem: the person who pays for the panels is rarely the person who pays the electricity bill. Every workable arrangement solves that first.

## The split-incentive problem

A tenant on a metered supply keeps 100% of the savings. A landlord who installs a ฿180,000 system and does not touch the rent has donated it. Nightly rentals are worse: guests stay three nights, run the air conditioning at 18°C, and never see a saving they could value.

So the question is not "does solar pay back on a villa?" — it is "which of these three structures are you in?"

## Structure 1 — Owner installs, rent adjusts

The cleanest for a long-term rental. The owner funds the system, the tenant gets a lower and more predictable electricity bill, and the rent rises by roughly the monthly saving minus a margin — in Krabi, commonly ฿2,000–฿3,500 a month on a 5 kWp system.

What makes it work:

- The villa is metered directly by PEA, in the tenant's name, so the saving is visible and verifiable.
- The lease is 2 years or more.
- The system is sized to the villa's actual daytime load (pool pump, AC), not to the roof.

What to write into the lease: who maintains the panels, who keeps the warranty documents, and what happens if the tenant leaves mid-term.

## Structure 2 — Tenant installs on a long lease

Rational only with a lease of five years or more and the owner's written consent. The tenant pays, the tenant saves, and the equipment is either removed or sold to the owner at the end. Put the removal and valuation clause in writing before installation, not after.

The utility paperwork is the practical obstacle: the meter is in the owner's name, so the owner usually has to co-sign the application. Landlords agree to this more often than tenants expect — it costs them nothing and improves the property.

## Structure 3 — Owner installs, utilities included

Common in nightly and monthly rentals where electricity is bundled into the rate. Here solar cuts the owner's operating cost directly: a 10 kWp system can remove ฿4,000–฿6,000 a month of air-conditioning load in high season. It also reduces the risk of a guest running the AC flat out, because the marginal cost to the owner falls.

This is the structure with the clearest financial case for short-stay villas — but only if occupancy is high enough. At 40% annual occupancy, a ฿300,000 system does not earn its keep.

## What does not work

- **Nightly rentals with a metered guest supply.** The guest sees no benefit and will not pay a premium for panels.
- **A system sized to the roof rather than the load.** A 20 kWp array on a villa that uses 900 kWh a month is a showpiece.
- **Skipping the metering conversation.** If the tenant pays a flat rate to the owner rather than PEA, the "savings" accrue to the owner and the whole pitch changes.

## The Krabi-specific bits

Salt air within a kilometre or two of the coast eats cheap mounting hardware and unsealed connectors. Monsoon season brings debris and, occasionally, a week of grey skies — production in September can run 25–30% below March. A rental system should be specified for both, not for a brochure.

Maintenance is the other landlord cost people forget: two to four cleanings a year, an annual inspection, and a named contact for the guest or the manager when the inverter shows a fault.
