On the Krabi coast the grid is mostly reliable and occasionally not. A tree branch, a monsoon squall, a transformer that gives up in April — the outages are short and unpredictable, which is worse for planning than long and scheduled.

A hybrid system solves that. But "add a battery" is not a specification, and the difference between a useful backup and an expensive ornament comes down to four decisions.

## 1. Which circuits stay on

The single most expensive mistake is assuming the whole house stays on. It does not, unless you buy a very large battery and a very large inverter.

A sensible backup panel in a Thai house:

- Lights and fans
- Fridge and freezer
- Internet router and CCTV
- Water pump (short duty cycles)
- One air-conditioner, in the bedroom, if the budget allows — air conditioning is 60–70% of the load, so this one item can double the battery

Leave the electric oven, the pool pump, the water heater and the second AC off the backup side. They can wait two hours.

## 2. How many kWh that is

Rough numbers for a two-person household:

| Load | Nightly consumption |
|---|---|
| Lights, fans, router, fridge | 2 – 3 kWh |
| \+ water pump | 3 – 4 kWh |
| \+ one bedroom AC (8 h) | 7 – 9 kWh |

So a 5 kWh battery covers essentials comfortably for one evening and most of a second. A 10 kWh unit covers essentials plus one AC. Sizing beyond that rarely earns its price — the grid comes back, and an oversized battery sits at 100% doing nothing.

## 3. What the inverter must do

Not every "hybrid" inverter provides backup. Some are hybrid only in the sense that they accept a battery for self-consumption. What you need is a unit with a dedicated backup output and a transfer time under 20 ms, so the router and the fridge do not notice the switch.

Also check the backup output's power rating, not just the array rating. A 5 kW inverter often backs up only 3–5 kW continuously, and starting an AC or a pump draws several times its running current.

## 4. What it costs and what it buys

Battery storage runs roughly ฿12,000 – ฿20,000 per kWh installed, plus the hybrid inverter premium of 15–30% over a grid-tie unit, plus the backup sub-panel and its wiring.

On tariff savings alone, a battery rarely pays back inside its warranty. Its value is: no spoiled food, no dark evening, no generator, no restarting the router, no pumping water by hand. For a family with a small child or a home office, that is worth paying for. For an empty holiday villa, usually not.

## When hybrid is the wrong answer

- The supply is stable and outages are minutes, not hours.
- The house is empty most of the year.
- The battery is being bought for "energy independence" — a grid-tied system with a small battery is not independence, it is a buffer.
- The load you want to back up is air conditioning for the whole house. At that point a generator is cheaper per kW, if you can tolerate it.

## The honest sizing conversation

We ask for three things: the last three bills, what must not stop during an outage, and how long outages usually last in your area. Those answers decide the battery in twenty minutes. The panel count comes after — a battery does not need a bigger array, it needs a defined job.
