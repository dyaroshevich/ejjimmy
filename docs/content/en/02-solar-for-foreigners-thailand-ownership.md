There is no rule in Thailand that stops a foreigner from owning a solar system. The friction is never the panels — it is the property paperwork and the electricity account. Here is how it plays out in practice on the Krabi coast.

## The short answer

You can buy, own and run a solar installation if you control the property and the meter. What the utility cares about is not your passport but who is named on the electricity account for that meter, and whether that person has the right to authorise work on the building.

## By property type

**Freehold condo.** Foreigners can own condo units outright within the 49% foreign quota of a building. But the roof is common property, managed by the juristic person. A single owner cannot punch through it. What you need is an AGM resolution allowing rooftop solar, plus the juristic person signing as the applicant for the common area — or a system on your own terrace/balcony within the unit's boundary, which is a much smaller and often disappointing option.

**Leasehold villa or land.** A registered leasehold runs up to 30 years. As the lessee you can install, but get the owner's written consent attached to the lease, and make sure the lease says who owns the equipment at the end of the term and who pays for removal. Without that clause, a 25-year panel warranty sits on a 3-year lease.

**Property held by a Thai company.** Straightforward — the company signs, the company claims the depreciation. This is the cleanest structure for a business or a rental portfolio.

**Renting long-term.** You need the owner's written permission and, usually, the owner as co-applicant on the utility paperwork, because the meter is in their name. Many landlords agree; almost none agree to pay for it.

## What the utility actually asks for

For a PEA or MEA application you will typically be asked for:

- Passport or Thai ID of the account holder
- House registration book (blue book) or the lease agreement plus owner consent
- The meter number and current tariff
- A single-line diagram and equipment datasheets from the installer

In Krabi this is handled by the local PEA office, and the quality of the process depends heavily on whether your installer has done it before. A missing yellow book copy can cost you two weeks.

## Permits for the building itself

A rooftop system on a house you control, with no structural change, normally does not need a building permit. Adding a ground-mounted array, raising the roof, or building a carport structure does. Check with the local Tessaban or OrBorTor before you order steel — this is the one area where a cheap installer's optimism becomes your problem.

## Three traps worth knowing

1. **The "free solar" lease.** A company installs at no cost and you pay for the electricity for 15–20 years. The rate is usually near the grid tariff, so you take the roof commitment without the savings, and selling the house becomes complicated.
2. **A system sized for the house you might build.** Over-sizing for a future extension sounds prudent; in practice the extra panels produce at a tariff you cannot consume, and the payback never arrives.
3. **No transfer clause.** If you sell, the buyer inherits the warranty only if the documents are transferable and the serial numbers are recorded. Ask for a commissioning file — it costs the installer an hour and saves the next owner a fight.

## What to do first

Get three things in one place: the property document that shows you can authorise work, the electricity bill with the meter number, and a photo of the roof from ground level. With those three, a survey can tell you in an hour whether the project is simple. Without them, nobody can quote honestly.
