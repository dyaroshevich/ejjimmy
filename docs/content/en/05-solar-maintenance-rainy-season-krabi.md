A solar system has no moving parts, which is why people assume it needs no attention. On the Krabi coast that assumption costs 5–10% of annual production and, occasionally, an inverter.

## What this climate actually does

- **Rain does not clean.** It sheets dust into streaks and leaves a film along the lower edge of each panel. Heavy monsoon rain helps, but the dry-season dust that arrives before it does more harm than the rain undoes.
- **Salt air corrodes.** Within roughly 1–3 km of the sea, unprotected steel, cheap rail clamps and unsealed connectors pit within a few years. Anodised aluminium and stainless fixings are not a premium — they are the baseline here.
- **Heat cuts output.** Panel temperature above 45°C costs 10–15% versus the datasheet rating. Good mounting clearance matters more in Krabi than in Europe.
- **Storms test the hardware.** Wind load and flying debris find weak cable ties, loose MC4 connectors and unclipped cable runs.

## The yearly routine

**Cleaning: two to four times a year.** Dry season (February–April) matters most. Clean early morning or after 17:00 — cold water on hot glass can crack a panel, and cleaning at noon in bare feet on a metal roof is how people get hurt. Water and a soft brush; no abrasive powder, no pressure washer near connectors.

**After the monsoon: a physical inspection.** Cracked glass, loose clamps, chafed DC cable, water in a junction box, rust on rails, a DC isolator that no longer turns freely.

**Once a year: electrical checks.** Torque on terminations, earth continuity, surge protection status, inverter error log. Most inverter faults announce themselves in the log weeks before they stop production.

**Every few years: a thermal look.** A hot-spot scan finds a failing panel or a bad connection that a visual inspection cannot. It is cheap compared with the production it recovers.

## Five failures worth catching early

1. **Burnt MC4 connectors.** The most common DC failure and the most dangerous. Usually an undersized or poorly crimped connector, made worse by salt air.
2. **A tripped or dead surge protector.** After a lightning season it may have done its job and now protects nothing. It is a consumable, not a permanent part.
3. **String imbalance.** One shaded or failed panel drags the whole string. Visible only in per-string data or on a thermal scan.
4. **Loose mounting.** Wind works fixings loose gradually; by the time it is visible, one corner has been moving for a season.
5. **Water ingress.** Junction boxes and roof penetrations. In this climate, a bad seal is a slow failure with a fast ending.

## Warranty claims need paperwork

Panel warranties run 12–25 years, inverters 5–10, workmanship typically 1–5. Every claim needs the same four things: model and serial numbers of the failed unit, the commissioning date, the installation report, and the production data showing the drop. If your installer did not hand you a commissioning file, ask for it now — three years later the company may not answer.

Note the expected degradation before you claim anything: panels lose about 0.5% of output per year by specification. A 3-year-old array producing 1.5% less than its first month is normal, not a defect.

## What not to do

- Do not pressure-wash connectors or junction boxes.
- Do not walk on a tile roof without a harness and a second person.
- Do not "fix" DC wiring yourself. A rooftop string carries lethal voltage in daylight and cannot be switched off at the panel.
- Do not clean with a hard broom. Micro-scratches on the glass become permanent soiling traps.
